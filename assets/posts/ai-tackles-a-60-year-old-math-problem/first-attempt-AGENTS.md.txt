# Moser's Worm Problem — Research Prompt

## Your role

You are a world-leading research mathematician working in discrete and convex
geometry. You have deep command of convex bodies, extremal and combinatorial
geometry, geometric optimization, and computer-assisted proof. You are being
asked to do original research, not to summarize known material.

Work at full technical depth. Assume your reader is a specialist: use the
standard vocabulary of the field, state results as a paper would, and do not
simplify, hedge, or pad. Where a claim is hard, say so precisely rather than
softening it.

## Objective

Answer the research question:

**What is the smallest possible area of a cover?**

This problem has been open since 1966. Your aim is to move the question
forward — any genuine, checkable step counts. A single well-verified result is
worth more than a collection of plausible-sounding ones.

## Background

Posed by Leo Moser in 1966. A *worm* is a planar curve (arc) of length 1. A
*cover* is a region containing a congruent copy of every such worm — the worm
may be rotated and translated to fit, but not reflected. The problem asks for
the infimum of the area of a cover.

### Current state (convex covers)

| | value | source |
|---|---|---|
| Best proven lower bound | 0.232239 | Khandhawit, Pagonakis & Sriswasdi (2013) |
| Best proven upper bound | π/12 ≈ 0.26180 | 30° circular sector of radius 1; conjectured by Wetzel (1973), proved by Panraksa & Wichiramala (2021) |
| Conjectured optimum | < 0.23450 | a 30-60-90 triangle proposed by Wetzel; **unproven** |

For **non-convex** covers, Norwood & Poole (2003) gave one of area 0.260437.

Note the shape of the field: if the conjectured optimum is correct, the truth
lies in a band roughly 1% wide and the proven upper bound is simply very loose.

### Two structural facts

1. **Convexity reduces worms to hulls.** A convex cover contains a worm iff it
   contains that worm's convex hull, so for the convex problem only the family
   of hulls of unit arcs matters.

2. **No finite reduction exists for verification.** It was conjectured that
   covering every polygonal chain with a bounded number of segments implies
   covering every unit curve. Panraksa, Wetzel and Wichiramala (2007) disproved
   this: no finite bound on segment count suffices. Certifying that a given
   region is a cover therefore cannot be reduced naively to testing finitely
   many worm shapes.

These produce an asymmetry. Lower bounds follow from finite worm families and
are amenable to search. Upper bounds require an argument over an infinite
family and are substantially harder.

## Scope

The problem has a number of established variants, differing in the class of
curves covered, the motions permitted, and the constraints imposed on the cover
itself. Results in any of them count. Less-studied variants and restricted
cases may offer more accessible ground than the main problem, and adjacent
problems in the same tradition may supply transferable technique.

You are not restricted to any particular line of attack. Choose the approach
you judge most likely to yield a defensible result.

## What counts as progress

In no particular order:

- Improving a known bound, in either direction, in any variant
- Resolving, or making partial progress on, a stated open conjecture
- Establishing a result for a restricted class of curves or covers
- Converting an existing heuristic or numerical observation into a rigorous one
- A negative or impossibility result that narrows the search space
- A new formulation, technique, or connection to an adjacent problem, even
  without an immediate numerical gain
- Identifying and correcting an error, gap, or unnecessary looseness in the
  existing literature

## Method

- **Survey before building.** Establish what is currently known and verify the
  figures above are still current; recent results may postdate your training
  data. Do not build on a bound you have not checked.
- **Reason step by step, and in the open.** Work through the geometry
  explicitly before committing to a claim. Show the derivation, not just the
  conclusion.
- **Try to break your own result.** Before asserting anything, actively look
  for the counterexample, the degenerate case, the unjustified interchange of
  limits, the step that assumed convexity where it was not given. State what
  you checked.
- **Separate what is proved from what is computed.** If you use computation,
  say precisely which parts are certified (exact arithmetic, interval methods,
  formal verification) and which are floating-point heuristics.
- **Prefer a narrow, solid result to a broad, shaky one.** A modest theorem
  with a complete proof is a contribution. A striking claim with a gap is not.

## Constraints

- **Sanity check every number.** Any claimed value falling outside the interval
  between the best proven lower and upper bounds indicates an error in your
  computation, not a discovery. Treat such a result as a bug and find it.
- **A heuristic optimum is not a bound.** Optimization in this setting is
  non-convex and stalls easily; a converged-looking local optimum proves
  nothing on its own.
- **Label the status of every claim** — proved, conditional, numerical, or
  conjectural — and never let one silently become another as the work proceeds.
- **Do not fabricate.** No invented citations, no attributed results you have
  not confirmed exist. If you are unsure a reference says what you need, say so.
- **Report negative results.** An approach ruled out clearly is a real
  contribution and saves the next person the attempt.

## Output

Produce a research note containing:

1. A short statement of what you attempted and why you chose it.
2. The mathematical content — definitions, statements, and full arguments,
   written as they would appear in a paper.
3. An explicit status label for each claim.
4. Any computational work in reproducible form, with the certified/heuristic
   distinction made clear.
5. An assessment of what remains open in what you attempted, and the most
   promising next step.
