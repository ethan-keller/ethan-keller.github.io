<problem> Work in the Euclidean plane R^2 with the standard metric. "Area" means Lebesgue measure.

Definitions:

A worm is a curve of length exactly 1: a continuous map gamma: [0,1] -> R^2 whose arc length (total variation) equals 1.
A congruent copy of a set S is g(S) for an isometry g of R^2. Isometries here include reflections (the full group E(2)).
A set K in R^2 covers a worm if some congruent copy of its trace is contained in K. K is a universal cover if it covers every worm.
The convex case restricts K to convex sets. Any convex universal cover of finite area is automatically a compact convex body up to closure, so you may work with compact convex bodies.

Define alpha* := inf { area(K) : K is a convex universal cover }.

Solve the problem completely: determine alpha* exactly and the convex universal covers that attain it, with a complete proof.

Conventions are yours to fix where the statement above leaves freedom, provided you state them explicitly and your final theorem is unambiguous.
</problem>

<publicly_known> This is the published state of knowledge, from memory, so verify every citation yourself before relying on it.

Leo Moser posed the problem in 1966.
Best published rigorous bounds for the convex case: 0.232239 <= alpha* <= 0.270911 Lower bound: Khandhawit, Pagonakis, Sriswasdi (2013). Upper bound: Wang (2006). Earlier convex covers: Gerriets and Poole (1974), Norwood, Poole, Laidacker (1992). Norwood and Poole (2003) gave a non-convex cover of area about 0.260437, which shows the convexity constraint matters.
Related literature exists on universal covers for arcs, "broadworms" (arcs maximizing minimal width), covers for closed curves, and the Lebesgue universal cover problem for sets of diameter 1.

Use the literature as a sanity check and a source of tools, not as a boundary. A claimed alpha* outside the interval above means either your argument or a published theorem is wrong. Treat that as your error until you have triple-checked and can exhibit the flaw in the published proof.

Do not assume the published approaches are the right ones. Sixty years of bounding by finitely many test curves has not closed the problem. You are free, and expected, to find a different road.
</publicly_known>

<definition_of_done> The task is finished when, and only when, all of the following hold.

PROOF.md (or a LaTeX source plus compiled PDF) exists in this directory and is a complete, self-contained proof of the solution. It states the main theorem first, then every definition, lemma, and proof in logical order. A competent mathematician who has never seen this project can check it line by line, by hand and without running anything, using only the document.
No gaps. Every step is either a standard textbook fact with a precise citation or is proved in full. "It is easy to see", "clearly", and "by a similar argument" are not permitted in load-bearing positions. Every case analysis lists all cases and proves the list is exhaustive.
Analytic proof, no computer assistance in load-bearing positions. Every load-bearing step is a mathematical argument a reader can follow with pen and paper. You may use computer algebra, numerics, plots, and simulation freely while searching, and you may use computer algebra to carry out or check a closed-form manipulation that appears in the document — expanding, factoring, differentiating, solving an equation, counting roots, verifying an identity — provided the manipulation is stated explicitly in the document and could in principle be reproduced by hand with patience. Not permitted in a load-bearing position: exhaustive or branch-and-bound search, grid or interval-arithmetic enumeration over a parameter space, numerically obtained certificates (LP, SDP, SOCP) whose validity rests on machine output, and any case analysis whose case list was generated by a program rather than derived by proof. Any computation quoted in the document is given with its input, its manipulation, and its result, and any script used to check it is included in the project. This constrains the form of the final argument, not how you explore, and it is never grounds for lowering the target: if a step is currently only verifiable by machine, it is an unproved lemma, and the task is to prove it.
Independent hostile refereeing. The proof has been attacked by at least three independent referee subagents who saw only the proof document, not your reasoning, and who were instructed to find a fatal flaw. Every objection has been resolved and the resolution recorded. A round with a surviving objection is not a pass.
Grading discipline. Every claim in your working notes carries one of these grades, and the final PROOF.md contains only claims graded THEOREM:
THEOREM: complete proof, refereed.
PROPOSITION: proof with a named gap.
CONJECTURE: believed, unproved.
FALSE: refuted, with the counterexample.
Final self-audit. Before declaring completion, reread the entire PROOF.md from the beginning as an adversary. Ask of every sentence: what would a hostile referee say? Only after the document survives your own attack, and the external referees, is the task complete.
</definition_of_done>

<principles>
Each rule comes with its reason so you can apply it in situations these words do not anticipate.

1. You do not stop, and you do not scale down. Partial results and well-supported conjectures are checkpoints: record them and keep going. Never reframe a checkpoint as a reasonable final goal. The reason: a partial result leaves the problem exactly as open as before, and the deliverable is the closed problem.

2. "Too hard" is not a permitted conclusion. Never write, think, or imply that the problem is unattainable or beyond current methods. When a route fails, name the exact lemma or assumption that broke, write the diagnosis down, and open new routes that avoid it. The reason: a failed route is information about the route, not about the problem, and a researcher who believes a problem is impossible stops generating ideas.

3. Anything is possible; build what does not exist. If the tool you need does not exist, build it. If the theory you need does not exist, create it. If the obstruction looks like it belongs to another field or discipline, go there and build the bridge. The reason: the great breakthroughs were bridges, and sixty years inside the obvious framework did not close this problem.

4. Reason from first principles. Start from the definitions in <problem>, not from published approaches. Derive everything you use from those definitions, and prove it rather than assume it. Question every "obvious" fact, including those in this document. The reason: inherited framings carry inherited blind spots.

5. Relentless in pursuit, ruthless in verification. Never giving up and never faking are the same virtue. A claim with a gap is a PROPOSITION, never a THEOREM, and you do not build on it as if it held. A refuted claim is recorded as FALSE with its counterexample, and the counterexample is mined for what the true statement must be. The reason: a hidden gap stops the search while the problem stays open, and only a correct proof is an answer.

6. Full effort, always. Do not conserve tokens, time, or agents, and do not shortcut a verification because it is tedious. The reason: the budget is unlimited so that rigor and coverage are never traded for cost.

7. Decide for yourself. Make every decision yourself, be critical, and record it with its reason in PROGRESS.md, and move on.
</principles>

<orchestration>
Use subagents and worflows aggressively and dynamically. The root/orchestrator agent synthesizes, challenges, redirects, and launches new rounds; it does not pursue one line alone.
Start with a genuinely diverse portfolio of independent approaches. Do not tell most agents which approach is currently favored, so early rounds do not converge on the same attractive but incomplete reduction.
Keep a registry of approach families in PROGRESS.md, grouped by the underlying idea rather than wording. When many agents converge on one family, redirect some to underexplored ones.
A route that ends at a lemma as strong as the original problem is not near completion, however elegant the reduction.
A route whose crux is an enumeration or search no reader could redo by hand is blocked, not finished; record the enumeration as an unproved lemma and keep going.
When a route stalls at a missing lemma of theorem strength, mark it blocked. Reassign agents to it only for a materially new mechanism, invariant, or construction.
Keep several incompatible routes alive across rounds. Cross-pollinate only after independent agents have developed them far enough to expose real strengths and gaps.
Run adversarial agents throughout, not only at the end. Maintain a growing checklist of failure modes they uncover and audit every candidate against it.
Require concrete returns from every agent: a lemma with proof, a construction, an equation, or a counterexample. Reject status reports, vague optimism, and claims that an unproved global statement is routine.
When a wave fails, launch the next one with fresh formulations. There is no last round until the proof survives audit.
</orchestration>
